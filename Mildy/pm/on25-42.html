Weak topics to target next session

Unit/precision traps: minutes vs hours, percent vs decimal

Two separate questions lost marks to the exact same shape of error — a fully correct method broken by not converting a value into the right form before using it. Q9's speed–time graph area (1450) needed ÷60 to turn km/h×min into real km; Q14b's profit equation used 34 instead of 0.34. Worth a standing habit of underlining any percentage or mismatched unit in a question before starting the working.

Applying given ratios/scale factors to parallel or similar quantities

Q18b treated CB as equal to OA despite the question explicitly stating OA:CB=4:3, and Q7b treated the parallelogram's slant side as its height without applying the cos36° factor the diagram gave (worth all 4 marks here, since no working was shown to earn partial credit either). Both are the same underlying habit — when a diagram or question gives a ratio or angle connecting two related lengths, it has to be used, not just the direction/shape copied across.

Finishing the final step

Q15c and Q21 both show fully correct method with all the hard work done, but the very last combining/calculating step was never carried out — 112 was boxed instead of computing 300×sin68° in Q15c, and Q21 stopped after finding both constants without substituting them into a final expression. Worth a check at the end of every multi-step question: does the boxed answer actually follow from the last line of working?

Transformation vocabulary: rotation vs reflection

Q8b named the single transformation mapping shape A onto shape B as a reflection in x=−2, when it's actually a 90° anticlockwise rotation about (−2,6) — the x=−2 detail suggests the centre of rotation was spotted but the transformation type itself wasn't checked. A quick visual test (does the shape flip/mirror, or does it turn while keeping the same orientation?) before naming the transformation would catch this.

Copying values accurately from table to graph

Q11b's table (part a) was completely correct, but two of the nine points plotted from it didn't match: (−2,−1) became (−2,1) — the negative sign was lost — and (1.5,3.38) became (1.5,3.8) — a digit dropped when transcribing the decimal. The curve itself was still drawn smoothly, so this isn't a plotting-skill issue, just a habit of double-checking each point against the table row by row before moving to the next.

Combined probability without replacement

Q16b mixed up which category's count to use (17 from C instead of 10 from D) and didn't reduce the denominator on the second pick, both suggesting the “choose 2 from a combined pool, without replacement” structure needs more practice — specifically identifying the right numerator for the actual event asked about, and the numerator-and-denominator-both-drop-by-one pattern.

Showing working, even for short final answers

Q12b (26 vs the correct 27, off by just 1°) and Q7b ($710.11 vs the correct ~$574) both had no working shown at all, which meant no partial credit was available for either — even though Q7b's wrong answer implies the final ×18 step was done correctly, examiners mark what's on the page, not what can be reverse-engineered from a number. A quick, minimal working line even for short answers protects marks when a small slip (or a bigger one) creeps in.
